About

I am a PhD candidate in Computer Science at the University of Geneva, working jointly with the Data Mining and Machine Learning group (DMML) at HES-SO Genève and the Kinesiology Laboratory of the University of Geneva and Geneva University Hospitals. My doctoral work is supervised by Prof. Alexandros Kalousis, Prof. Stéphane Marchand-Maillet and Prof. Stéphane Armand, within the SNSF-funded Metathesis project.

My research lies at the intersection of machine learning and biomechanics. Human movement is, in principle, a high-dimensional system: hundreds of muscles, dozens of joints and considerable variability between individuals. In practice, it is governed by a limited number of underlying patterns, and my work focuses on methods that identify and exploit them, including latent representations, muscle synergies and geometric approaches. I place equal emphasis on clinical relevance: a model of gait is only useful if it remains reliable on real patients and informs actual clinical decisions, not only in simulation.

In parallel, I co-founded a-gO, where I serve as Chief Technology Officer and lead the translation of gait research into a clinical product. The a-gO system relies on computer vision and markerless pose estimation to reconstruct gait from video, and on explainable AI to make the contribution of each aspect of gait to a given result transparent to clinicians. A five-minute walk on a standard treadmill, without wearables or electrodes, yields a motor signature of more than 200 gait parameters for the longitudinal monitoring of neurocognitive health. The same acquisition engine underlies a-gO Research, a multi-camera motion capture system for research laboratories.

Research

Walking poses a considerable control problem: hundreds of muscles and dozens of joints must be coordinated within fractions of a second. Yet the resulting behaviour is highly structured. My research develops machine learning methods that capture this structure and apply it in two complementary settings, physics-based simulation and clinical practice.

Musculoskeletal control

Controlling a full musculoskeletal model with reinforcement learning remains a difficult problem: each muscle constitutes an action dimension, and unstructured exploration rarely produces coordinated locomotion. I study muscle synergies as the action space of the agent: the policy acts on a small set of primitives that a weight matrix maps onto the full set of muscles. This formulation makes learning tractable and aligns the resulting control with prevailing hypotheses on how the nervous system organises movement.

Clinical decision support

Treatment decisions for children with cerebral palsy, such as surgery, botulinum toxin injections or orthoses, rely heavily on clinical gait analysis, in which experts interpret dozens of joint-angle curves. In collaboration with the Kinesiology Laboratory at the Geneva University Hospitals, I develop latent representations of pathological gait: low-dimensional spaces in which patients with similar gait patterns lie close to one another. These representations make gait comparable across patients and provide a basis for supporting treatment selection.
